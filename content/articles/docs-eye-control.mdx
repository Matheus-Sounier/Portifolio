When I started experimenting with webcam interaction, I wanted something simple but responsive enough to control a game without touching the keyboard.

The idea I ended up building was surprisingly straightforward: instead of detecting a full facial expression, I only needed to know whether the eye was open or closed. Every blink would simulate pressing the **Space** key.

---

## Why I Used MediaPipe

The first problem was face tracking.

I didn't want to train my own landmark detector or rely on classical computer vision techniques that usually become unstable with different lighting conditions.

MediaPipe already provides a high-quality face mesh with **468 facial landmarks**, so instead of solving face detection myself, I could focus only on the blink detection logic.

Once a face is detected, every frame already contains the coordinates of the eye landmarks.

## Measuring an Eye Blink

Knowing where the eye is isn't enough.

I still needed a way to decide whether it was open or closed.

Instead of comparing raw pixel values, I calculate the distance between specific eyelid landmarks. As the eyelids move closer together, that distance decreases.

```python
upper = landmarks[159]
lower = landmarks[145]

eye_distance = abs(upper.y - lower.y)
```

When the value drops below a threshold, I consider the eye closed.

```text
Eye Distance

High --- Eye Open

Low  --- Eye Closed
```

The nice part is that this doesn't depend on colors, shadows, or textures—only on the landmark positions estimated by MediaPipe.

---

## Turning a Blink into a Keyboard Input

Detecting a closed eye wasn't the final goal.

I wanted the blink to behave exactly like pressing the keyboard.

When the eye closes, the project sends a **Space key down** event.

When the eye opens again, the key is released.

---

## BlendShapes Help Too

Besides the landmarks, MediaPipe also estimates facial BlendShapes.

These values describe facial movements such as blinking, smiling, raising eyebrows, and many others.

Although the landmark distances already worked well, having BlendShapes available made it easier to experiment with different approaches and compare their stability.

---

## Watching the Detection Live

One thing I always like while developing computer vision projects is visual feedback.

Instead of printing numbers in the terminal, I used CVZone's LivePlot to visualize the eye measurements in real time.

![Live plot](/articles/img/liveplot.png)

The graph immediately shows whether the threshold makes sense. If the signal becomes noisy or unstable, it's much easier to notice visually than by looking at raw numbers.

---

A normal blink only lasts a fraction of a second, so choosing a threshold that's too high causes false positives.

Lighting conditions, camera angle, glasses, and even how close the user sits to the webcam all influence the measurements.

Most of my time wasn't spent writing code—it was spent tweaking those small details until the detection felt natural.
